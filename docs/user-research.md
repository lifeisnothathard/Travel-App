Honestly? **You don't need to find 100 strangers.** 😂 For a December MVP, you can get your first testers for basically RM0 if you approach it correctly.

The trick is **don't ask people to “test my app.”** Ask them to help with a very specific travel problem.

### 1. Start with people you already have access to

You probably already have potential testers around you:

* university/college students
* friends who travel to Singapore/Japan/Korea/Thailand
* coworkers/interns
* family members who travel
* Malaysian travel groups
* people in your existing social media circles

You don't need them to be software people.

In fact, **non-technical travellers are probably better testers.**

You want someone to say:

> “Why the hell would I use this instead of Google Maps?”

because that's valuable feedback. 😭

---

### 2. Find testers through Malaysian communities

You can post something like:

Hi! 👋 I'm currently building a small travel app as a personal project and I'm looking for a few people who travel regularly to help me test it.

It's completely free, and you don't need any technical knowledge.

I'm specifically looking for people who have travelled to places like Japan, Korea, Singapore, Thailand, etc. and are willing to spend around 10–15 minutes trying out an early version and giving honest feedback.

You don't need to download anything immediately — I'll explain what I'm testing first.

If you're interested, just comment/DM me! Thank you 🥹

You could post that in relevant **Facebook groups, Reddit communities, Discord servers, university groups, or travel communities**, subject to each group's rules.

---

### 3. Don't give them a blank app

This is REALLY important.

Don't send:

> “Here's my app. Tell me what you think.”

You'll get:

> “Looks nice!”

💀

Instead, give them a task.

For example, if you build the **confusing-station-exit** idea:

> **Scenario:** You're arriving at Shinjuku Station and need to reach a café outside Exit B12.
>
> Without asking me for help, use the app to figure out where you should go.
>
> Tell me:
>
> 1. Where you think you're supposed to go
> 2. What confused you
> 3. Whether you'd trust the instructions
> 4. What you'd normally use instead

Now you're collecting **actual usability evidence**.

---

### 4. You can compensate them without paying money

People will often participate because they're interested.

Offer something tiny:

* early access
* “founding tester” credit
* their name in a credits page (if they want it)
* ability to influence features
* free access to the finished app
* a cute digital thank-you
* raffle/gift card **if you eventually have a small budget**

But don't make payment necessary.

Your pitch can literally be:

> **“I'm a student building this independently and I'm looking for 10 people to break it before I release it.”**

That's actually more appealing than pretending it's some giant startup.

---

### 5. And here's the REALLY useful part for your portfolio

Keep records of the testing.

Make a simple spreadsheet:

| Tester | Travel background | Problem encountered                   | Feedback                     | Change made        |
| ------ | ----------------- | ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------ |
| T01    | Japan             | Couldn't understand exit instructions | Wanted landmark reference    | Added landmark     |
| T02    | Korea             | Too many steps                        | Wanted one-screen directions | Simplified flow    |
| T03    | Singapore         | Didn't trust route                    | Wanted source/time info      | Added verification |
| T04    | Thailand          | ...                                   | ...                          | ...                |

Now you've got **user research + usability testing + iteration evidence**.

That is WAY stronger than saying:

> “My friends tested my app.”

---

### And you actually have a nice timeline

Since you're aiming for **end of December**:

**October**
→ Choose ONE problem
→ Interview ~10 potential users
→ Prototype

**November**
→ Build MVP
→ First 5 testers
→ Fix major problems
→ Second testing round

**December**
→ Polish
→ 10–20 real users
→ Document results
→ Release MVP
→ Put everything on GitHub/portfolio

You don't need hundreds of users.

**10 people who genuinely experience the problem > 100 people who download your app and never open it.**

And if we choose the problem carefully, we can design the app so that **finding those first 10 testers is actually easy** rather than building something first and desperately hunting for users afterward.
